# Grooming an older dog safely

*[Intent line]* Your dog is getting older, and the grooming routine that used to be easy now seems harder for them, or for you. Here's how to adapt it.

*[Byline]* By Grooming Dogs · Updated *[publish date]*

*[Scope note]*
- **How often to groom an older dog:** see [adjusting the grooming schedule](/how-often-should-you-groom-your-dog/).
- **If your dog has become fearful of grooming:** see [if your dog resists](/grooming-an-anxious-dog/).

*[Lead image `lead-senior` (credit line only)]*

*[Short answer box]*
**Short answer:**
- **Old age isn't a disease** **[S1]**, and your older dog may not need any changes to their grooming.
- **If grooming has become harder,** keep sessions short with breaks, put your dog on a non-slip surface, let them sit, lie down or shift position, and use a soft brush on thin skin **[S1][S2]**.
- **Use grooming time to notice lumps and changes,** and have anything new checked by your vet **[S1][S2]**.
- **If your dog suddenly resists being handled or seems sore, see your vet.** Changes that look like "just getting old" can be pain **[S1][S3]**.

## What changes as dogs age

**There's no fixed age** when a dog becomes "senior". Veterinary guidelines describe a senior dog as being in roughly the last quarter of its expected lifespan, which varies between breeds and sizes **[S1]**.

**Old age is not a disease.** Not every older dog declines in the same way **[S1]**. Your dog may need no changes at all. But some older dogs find grooming harder:
- **Movement:** some older dogs move less easily or feel stiff, and pain and discomfort become more common in later life **[S1]**.
- **Skin:** some older dogs have thinner skin that cuts or bruises more easily **[S2]**.
- **Lumps and growths:** these become more common, and a vet can't always tell a harmless lump from a serious one just by looking at it **[S1]**.
- **Pain can look like aging:** owners often put changes down to normal aging when the real cause may be pain, and the signs can be subtle **[S1]**.

That last point matters for grooming. If your dog has started to resist being brushed or handled, don't assume it's just age (see "What to watch for while grooming" below).

## Adapting the routine

- **Keep sessions short.** Some older dogs can't stand comfortably for long **[S2]**.
- **Take frequent breaks.** Stop to stroke your dog or let them rest **[S2]**.
- **Split the work.** Brush one day, check nails and ears another. Nothing has to happen in one go.
- **Let your dog move.** Allow them to shift position or move around during the session **[S2]**.
- **Watch for "that's enough".** If your dog tries to leave or starts panting, they may be stressed or sore. Let them tell you when they've had enough **[S2]**.
- **Reward along the way.** Give small treats throughout the session **[S2]**.

How often each task needs doing still depends on your dog's coat. See [adjusting the grooming schedule](/how-often-should-you-groom-your-dog/).

## Handling and positioning

*[What you'll need box. If affiliate links are added later, the inline disclosure line goes at the top of this box, before the first link. **Never link supplements, joint products or calming products.**]*

**What you'll need**
- **Grip underfoot:** a bath mat, rug or yoga mat for better footing **[S1][S2]**.
- **Lifting help:** for a larger dog, a fitted, padded harness can help you lift and support them **[S1]**.
- **A soft brush.** See [choosing a brush](/dog-grooming-tools/).
- **Treats.**

**How to position an older dog:**
- **Groom on a non-slip surface,** with your dog standing or lying down, whichever is more comfortable **[S2]**.
- **Consider grooming at floor level** rather than lifting your dog onto a table. Veterinary guidance for older dogs suggests soft surfaces, or examining them off the table, for those with mobility problems **[S1]**.
- **Let your dog change position.** Lying down for a long time can put pressure on the hips or spine **[S2]**.
- **Move legs and joints gently,** and avoid stretching a leg further than your dog is comfortable with **[S1]**.
- **Support the whole body when lifting.** For a larger dog, a padded harness helps **[S1]**.
- **Don't hold your dog down.** If they can't be groomed without force, stop (see the callout).

*[Safety callout: "Stop and reassess"]* If your dog cries out, flinches at one spot, or can't get comfortable in any position, stop. Pain is a reason to see your vet, not to work around. If your dog is frightened rather than sore, see [if your dog resists](/grooming-an-anxious-dog/).

## Bathing an older dog

The bathing method doesn't change. The setup does:
- **Grip in the bath:** use a non-slip mat in the tub or shower, so your dog doesn't slip **[S1][S2]**.
- **Getting in and out:** help your dog in and out rather than letting them jump. Support a larger dog's body with a padded harness **[S1]**.
- **If standing is hard,** let your dog sit or lie on the non-slip mat while you wash them **[S2]**.
- **Dry thoroughly,** and keep your dog somewhere warm until they're completely dry.
- **Between baths,** a spot cleaner, waterless shampoo or pet wipe can deal with a single dirty patch without a full bath **[S5]**.

If your dog can't be bathed safely at home, a groomer or your vet's practice may be able to help. The full method is in [bathing technique](/bathing-your-dog/).

## Nail care for older dogs

Nails matter for footing. Long nails can reduce traction and affect how a dog stands and walks **[S4]**, and good grip is especially important for a dog who's already a little unsteady.
- **Check the nails as part of your routine.** How often they need trimming depends on your dog. See [adjusting the grooming schedule](/how-often-should-you-groom-your-dog/).
- **Trim the hair growing between the paw pads.** Long hair there can make an older dog slip on hard floors **[S2]**. See [trimming at home](/dog-haircuts-and-trimming/).
- **Choose a comfortable position.** If standing on three legs is hard, try lying your dog on their side on a non-slip mat.
- **Take your time.** Do a few nails, rest, then do the rest.

How to find the quick and trim safely: see [trimming nails](/trimming-dog-nails/).

## What to watch for while grooming

Grooming is a good chance to look your dog over. As you work, check for mats, lumps, hot spots and anything stuck in the coat. Pay attention to between the toes, under the tail, behind the ears and in the mouth **[S2]**.

*[See-a-vet callout]* **Contact your vet if you notice:**
- **A new lump or growth,** or one that has changed. A vet can't always tell a harmless lump from a serious one by sight, so have it checked rather than waiting to see **[S1]**.
- **Sores,** including pressure sores on the hips or elbows **[S2]**.
- **Skin or coat changes** you haven't seen before, or hair that isn't growing back.
- **Sensitivity in one spot,** or a sudden change in how your dog tolerates being handled. A sudden change in an adult dog's behavior can be an early sign of a health problem, including pain **[S3]**, and owners can easily mistake pain for aging **[S1]**.

**Brushing near lumps:** use a soft brush, because a slicker brush with metal pins can nick a wart or lump **[S2]**.

If something looks wrong after a groom, see [problems after grooming](/dog-grooming-problems/).

## Working with a groomer

Older dogs, especially those with long coats, can benefit from professional grooming **[S2]**.

**Finding a groomer:** look for a gentle groomer who is happy to work slowly. Veterinary guidance lists gentle groomers among the useful resources for owners of older pets **[S1]**. See [choosing a groomer](/choosing-a-dog-groomer/).

**Tell the groomer about:**
- stiffness or sore spots;
- lumps;
- any health conditions;
- how long your dog can comfortably stand.

Ask whether they can work in shorter stints, with breaks.

**Heavy matting:** if brushing out the mats would be painful for your dog, it may be kinder to have a groomer clip the coat **[S2]**. That's a decision for the groomer. Don't cut mats out at home (see [dealing with mats](/brushing-your-dog/#dealing-with-mats)).

**Health limits:** if your dog has significant health or mobility problems, talk to your vet before booking.

**Sedation:** whether sedation for grooming is appropriate for your dog is a decision for your vet.

## Common questions

*[FAQ: Elementor Accordion with FAQ Schema ON; question titles set to H3. Rank Math FAQ schema OFF.]*

### My old dog can't stand for a bath. What are my options?

You have several options:
- **Let your dog sit or lie down** on a non-slip mat in the tub or shower **[S2]**.
- **Support a larger dog** with a padded harness when lifting them in and out **[S1]**.
- **Spot-clean dirty patches** with a pet spot cleaner or wipes between baths **[S5]**.
- **Get help** from a groomer or your vet's practice if a full bath at home isn't safe.

If your dog seems to be in pain, see your vet first.

### Should I still brush a dog with thin skin?

Yes, gently. Brushing still helps keep the coat comfortable. Use a soft brush rather than one with stiff metal pins, which can be too harsh on thin skin and can nick warts or lumps **[S2]**. Keep sessions short, and check the skin as you go.

### My senior dog's coat isn't growing back after a clip. Should I worry?

Mention it to your vet. Coat that doesn't regrow, or new thinning or bald patches, is worth having checked rather than guessing at the cause.
